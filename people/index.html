When Wanting Changes

The medication can change your appetite. It cannot tell you what to do with that change.

GLP-1 medicines can make food quieter, hunger different and weight loss possible in ways that once felt unreachable. But biological change can also unsettle habits, pleasure, identity, relationships and the stories we have been taught about willpower and body size.

A public Social Clinic

Weight can change faster than the story you have about it.

What happens when hunger changes, but the rest of you does not change at the same speed?

When Wanting Changes is being designed for people who want to understand what can happen psychologically and socially when GLP-1 treatment alters appetite, food noise, weight and everyday life.

It is not about becoming the “perfect patient”. It is about making sense of an experience that medicine can cause but cannot interpret for you.

What it explores

The parts nobody puts on the prescription.

Food noise

What does it mean when thoughts about food become quieter — and what does that reveal about appetite, agency and self-control?

Habit without hunger

Why can old patterns persist even when appetite changes? Eating has always been more than a biological response to hunger.

Body and identity

What happens when your body changes faster than your sense of yourself, or when other people suddenly treat you differently?

Stigma and deservingness

Why do people still talk about weight loss as if suffering makes it morally legitimate — and medication somehow makes it cheating?

Relationships

Changes in eating and body size can alter routines, intimacy, attention and the roles people have grown used to playing.

Maintenance and uncertainty

What does long-term treatment mean psychologically and socially, especially when nobody can promise exactly how the story ends?

Clear boundaries

What this isn't.

Education about treatment is not the same thing as treating you.

This course will not tell you whether you should take a GLP-1 medicine, which medicine to use, what dose to take, or how your treatment should be managed.

Those are clinical decisions for you and an appropriately qualified health professional.

The course is about understanding the experience around treatment: wanting, eating, identity, stigma, relationships and change.

Created by

Dr Andrew Dickson

Andrew is an academic, writer and educator whose work crosses health, sociology, organisation studies and psychoanalytic theory.

His doctoral research examined the psychological and social dimensions of weight loss, appetite and weight-anxiety, including his own experience of pharmacologically mediated weight loss.

He created The Social Clinic to make serious ideas about health, medicine and human experience useful outside the university.
