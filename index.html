Beyond the Prescription

GLP-1 Medicines, Weight, Stigma and the Changing Clinical Encounter

3 hoursLive & onlineAotearoa New ZealandFor health professionals

GLP-1 medicines have transformed what medicine can do about weight. Have they transformed how we think about it?

An evidence-based, interactive masterclass examining what pharmacology alone cannot explain: the history of weight-loss medicine, appetite and desire, weight stigma, willpower and responsibility, patient experience, cultural context, health equity, and the changing relationship between clinician and patient.

What you'll explore

Beyond pharmacology.

Six connected conversations move from the history of weight-loss medicine to the consultation room—and ask what changes when appetite itself becomes medically alterable.

01 / HISTORY

How did we get here?

From appetite suppressants and Fen-Phen to contemporary incretin therapies: what earlier weight-loss medicines taught clinicians and patients.

02 / APPETITE

Willpower, biology & food noise

What happens to familiar ideas about discipline and responsibility when hunger and wanting can change pharmacologically?

03 / STIGMA

The moral economy of weight

Why losing weight may not end stigma—and why the method of weight loss can itself become morally charged.

04 / CONTEXT

What walks into the room?

Biography, whānau, culture, inequality, previous treatment and the assumptions carried by both patient and clinician.

05 / DESIRE

When wanting changes

Food is more than fuel. Explore pleasure, identity, comfort, prohibition and desire when patients say: “I just don't think about food anymore.”

06 / PRACTICE

Beyond the prescription

Translate the theory into a more reflective, non-stigmatising and patient-centred weight-management consultation.

Why The Social Clinic?

Biology is indispensable. It is also incomplete.

Patients don't arrive in consulting rooms as collections of physiological variables.

They arrive with biographies, relationships, expectations, fears, identities and histories. Clinicians bring histories and assumptions of their own.

The Social Clinic develops professional education at precisely that intersection: where biology meets biography, and medicine meets meaning.

Course director

Dr Andrew Dickson

Social theory made clinically useful.

Andrew is a Senior Lecturer at the University of Otago whose work crosses health sociology, psychoanalytic theory, organisation studies and the biological sciences.

He holds a PhD examining the weight-loss industry through Lacanian psychoanalysis, a Master's degree in Organisation Studies, and undergraduate training spanning biochemistry and business. He has more than twenty years' experience in university teaching and research.

His work asks a deceptively simple question: what becomes visible in healthcare when we look beyond the biomedical account?

Evidence & independence

Independent by design.

The Social Clinic's educational material is developed from peer-reviewed scholarship and current clinical evidence. Relevant disagreement and uncertainty are made explicit.

The Social Clinic does not accept pharmaceutical sponsorship for its core educational courses. No medicine or manufacturer is promoted.

The aim is not to tell clinicians what to prescribe. It is to help them understand more fully the clinical encounters in which prescribing occurs.
